## Mission

Implement the next substantial visual-development pass for three existing AV Gen scenes:

1. **Cymatic World:** significantly increase the amount, variety, and sophistication of music-driven visual activity; restore the rich spatial gradients from an earlier version of the scene; eliminate visible dead space and back walls.
2. **Resonant Silk:** transform the environment into an integrated, continuously animated visual world; develop a more translucent, luminous material; substantially expand the visual vocabulary that communicates audio and MIDI.
3. **Wave Form Dimension:** preserve the existing geometry, composition, and excellent audio/MIDI responsiveness while making a restrained improvement to lighting, translucency, and luminous edges.

Additionally, investigate and address the performance problems affecting all three scenes when audio and MIDI are enabled and output is being projected to an external display.

This is a targeted art and optimization pass. It is not authorization to redesign all three scenes from scratch, replace the renderer, or introduce a new general-purpose effects framework.

The guiding principle is:

**Increase the amount of meaningful visual information generated by the music, not merely the number of effects rendered.**

The finished scenes should exhibit coordinated motion, evolving geometry, traveling light, secondary ripples, fine details, and deliberate temporal changes. These behaviors should feel like manifestations of the underlying visual world and its response to music—not a collection of unrelated audio-reactive gimmicks.

# 2. Shared Artistic Direction: More Musical Visual Language

Cymatic World and Resonant Silk are not yet communicating enough of the music visually. Their current behavior too often amounts to geometry morphing, surface movement, color changes, and occasional highlights.

We need a richer visual vocabulary.

Develop coordinated layers of activity, with different spatial scales, response times, and visual roles.

## 2.1 The required layers of visual activity

Consider these layers as a design vocabulary. Implement the ones that make sense for each scene rather than indiscriminately adding everything.

### Layer A — Primary structural response

This is the main geometry's response to music.

Examples:

- Large resonant deformations.
- Changes in dominant wave amplitude.
- Flowing folds that tighten or relax.
- Major modal patterns that emerge and decay.
- Large wavefronts propagating through the scene.

These changes establish the overall musical gesture.

They should be immediately perceptible when the music changes.

### Layer B — Secondary ripples and sub-waves

Add smaller structures that ride on the main geometry.

Examples:

- Ripples traveling across broad surfaces.
- Secondary waves propagating through larger wavefronts.
- Fine oscillations along resonant ridges.
- Localized disturbances triggered by transients.
- Smaller wave patterns that emerge as a primary deformation settles.

These details must be spatially connected to the primary geometry.

Avoid simply adding another independent noise texture. Secondary motion should respond to the underlying surface, field, or wave structure.

### Layer C — Traveling illumination

Introduce light phenomena that move through the geometry and environment.

Examples:

- Luminous scans traveling along ridges.
- Narrow bands of light sweeping across surfaces.
- Broader illumination waves propagating through the scene.
- Light traveling along flowing folds.
- Localized brightening as waves intersect.
- Resonant regions that illuminate in sequence.

These effects should be coordinated with the scene's geometry and spatial coordinates.

A traveling light scan should visibly move through the world, not merely change the overall brightness of the scene.

### Layer D — Fine glimmers and optical details

Use small, controlled events to enrich the image.

Examples:

- Fine glimmers along selected ridges.
- Small highlights that travel across curved surfaces.
- Brief luminous accents at wave intersections.
- Subtle interference flickers.
- Small, spatially coherent pulses of emission.

Avoid turning these into random sparkle particles or independent flashing points.

They should reinforce the geometry and the motion.

### Layer E — Environmental response

The surrounding environment must participate in the music.

Examples:

- Background color fields shifting in response to sustained energy.
- Distant waves responding more slowly than foreground waves.
- Broad light sweeps crossing the entire environment.
- Subtle environmental pulses following musical phrases.
- Changes in the density or visibility of distant structures.
- Low-intensity interference patterns moving behind the main geometry.

The background should not be a separate decorative object. It should behave like another scale of the same visual system.

### Layer F — Phrase-level evolution

The scene should develop over a longer musical passage.

Examples:

- A gradual change in the dominant resonant pattern.
- A slowly evolving color relationship.
- A shift in the balance between large-scale and fine-scale motion.
- A wave system becoming more intricate during an energetic passage.
- A gradual return to a calmer configuration after sustained activity.

These changes should persist long enough to create a visual arc.

Do not switch randomly between unrelated visual states every few seconds.

## 2.2 Make the layers work together

The layers must be coordinated.

For example, a bass transient might produce a broad structural deformation. A secondary wave could propagate outward from that deformation, a luminous scan could travel along the resulting ridge, and a subtle environmental response could follow afterward.

The entire sequence should feel like one event with several consequences.

It should not look like five unrelated parameters reacting independently to the same audio peak.

Develop a small number of convincing interactions first. Expand the vocabulary only after those interactions work visually.

# 3. Audio and MIDI Mapping: Expressive Without Becoming Chaotic

Use AV Gen's existing audio-analysis, signal-bus, modulation, resonator, and MIDI systems.

Do not create duplicate audio analysis or a separate input-processing architecture for these effects.

## 3.1 Different signals should produce different visual behaviors

Where the existing signal system provides suitable inputs, establish differentiated responses.

Possible starting points:

| Musical input | Potential visual response |
|---|---|
| Sustained low-frequency energy | Broad deformation, large wave amplitude, or expansion of a resonant structure |
| Midrange energy | Secondary ripples, medium-scale interference, or more intricate folds |
| High-frequency energy | Fine surface vibration, delicate glimmers, or narrow traveling illumination |
| Strong transient | A localized wave impulse, traveling scan, or briefly intensified resonant response |
| Sustained energetic passage | Greater overall motion complexity and stronger environmental participation |
| Quiet passage | Reduced excitation with residual motion and gentle light evolution |
| MIDI note or mapped control | A discrete excitation, selected mode response, or controllable visual event |

These are suggestions, not mandatory fixed mappings.

Inspect the existing signal outputs and choose mappings that suit the scene and the actual musical material.

## 3.2 Avoid direct, unfiltered modulation of everything

Do not connect every visual parameter directly to the instantaneous audio signal.

Use the existing smoothing and envelope mechanisms to produce appropriate response characteristics.

Different behaviors should have different time constants:

- Large geometry: controlled attack and release, with persistence.
- Secondary ripples: quicker response but limited amplitude.
- Light scans: a short trigger followed by continuous spatial propagation.
- Fine glimmers: quick, localized accents with restrained intensity.
- Environmental evolution: slow, sustained response.
- Phrase-level changes: gradual transitions and long-term state.

A high-frequency input should not make the entire scene twitch.

A bass transient should not instantly alter every color, material, background, and camera parameter.

## 3.3 Make transient effects spatially propagate

For appropriate effects, an event should create a traveling disturbance rather than a one-frame flash.

Possible approaches include:

- An expanding wavefront.
- A moving band defined in surface coordinates.
- A damped response moving along a surface.
- A traveling phase or illumination field.
- A localized excitation that disperses into secondary waves.

Use persistent state and continuous time progression.

Do not reset a scan's phase arbitrarily every frame or regenerate its position from a noisy audio value.

The visual event should have a beginning, propagation, and decay.

## 3.4 Keep MIDI behavior intact

Preserve existing MIDI mappings and live controls.

Do not add dozens of new user-facing parameters simply because more internal effects are available.

Prefer a compact set of useful controls or use existing modulation paths to control the new behavior.

Do not break the existing live workflow to accommodate the art pass.

# 5. Resonant Silk — Integrated Environment, Translucent Material, and Rich Musical Motion

Do not use another isolated ribbon as the primary background solution.

Instead, design an environmental field that shares the same underlying visual language as the main flowing structures.

The background should be generated from a coherent spatial system rather than from a separate decorative object.

Possible approaches include:

- A continuous procedural field with broad flowing gradients.
- Distant, low-detail flowing structures that emerge from the same field as the foreground.
- Large-scale wave motion that continues behind and around the main forms.
- A layered field of flowing color and illumination.
- Distant surface structures that fade into the environment.
- Subtle, spatially coherent interference patterns.
- Slow environmental waves that respond to sustained musical energy.

Choose an approach that integrates naturally with the current geometry and rendering pipeline.

### The environment must be animated

It cannot be a static background with a slight color change.

Introduce continuous movement at an environmental scale.

The environment can move more slowly than the foreground, but its temporal behavior must be visible in rendered sequences.

The background and foreground should share related motion and color logic while retaining different detail densities and visual roles.

### Avoid

- A single ribbon behind the scene.
- Several duplicated ribbons at different angles.
- A flat gradient that merely fills the black space.
- A static skybox with unrelated colors.
- An environment that uses the same bright intensity as the foreground.
- A noisy field that competes with the primary forms.

The goal is an immersive environment, not a decorative backdrop.

## 5.2 Improve the spatial structure

Retain the strongest existing flowing geometry, but develop a more convincing sense of depth.

Establish distinct foreground, middle-ground, and background layers.

The primary forms should remain dominant.

- Overlap.
- Depth-dependent color and brightness.
- Differentiated motion speeds.
- Controlled differences in detail density.
- Subtle environmental illumination.
- Intentional negative space.

## 5.3 Develop a translucent, light-responsive material

Explore a more translucent appearance for the silk-like surfaces.

The desired result is a rich, luminous material with some sense of light passing through or diffusing within it, without losing the geometry's form.

Investigate the renderer's actual support for:

- Alpha blending.
- Transmission or transparency.
- Fresnel response.
- Emission.
- Environment lighting.
- Two-sided shading.
- Backlighting.
- Depth-aware effects.
- Other efficient approximations of translucency.

Use the most appropriate implementation supported by the engine.

Do not introduce expensive physical transmission or multiple transparent rendering passes without first measuring their cost.

### Desired appearance

- The material retains its saturated base color.
- Thinner or favorably oriented regions can appear brighter or more translucent.
- Folds reveal subtle variations in light and color.
- Backlighting can reveal the structure without turning it into a flat silhouette.
- Edges can catch light without becoming uniformly neon.
- Deeper folds remain visually legible.
- Highlights do not erase the underlying color.

The material should feel like an authored luminous silk-like substance, not clear plastic, polished rubber, or a transparent sheet of glass.

### Layer the optical response

Where appropriate, combine:

1. Rich base coloration.
2. Broad diffuse or equivalent surface response.
3. Subtle translucency or backlighting.
4. Selective edge illumination.
5. Restrained emission.
6. Controlled specular reflection.

Do not maximize all six simultaneously.

The material should have depth and variation, with a clear visual hierarchy between broad color, folds, and bright details.

### Protect against transparency artifacts

If actual transparency is used, check sorting, depth behavior, overlapping layers, and any renderer limitations.

If those limitations create distracting artifacts or excessive cost, prefer a stable shader-based approximation.

Visual coherence matters more than physically accurate but unstable transparency.

## 5.4 Give the flowing geometry more musical expression

The main geometry needs a more sophisticated range of responses.

Do not discard the existing resonator system if it already provides a useful foundation.

Instead, layer new behaviors around it.

### Primary flow

Improve the main flowing forms' ability to change curvature, tension, and amplitude in response to music.

Add smaller waves that travel along or across the flowing surfaces.

### Sub-waves

### Traveling light pulses

Develop moving illumination that travels along or through the silk.

Examples:

- A luminous pulse propagating along a fold.
- A band of light traveling through a broad surface.
- A wave of increased translucency or emission.
- A narrow highlight that moves along a curved ridge.
- A light disturbance that crosses between adjacent layers.

Coordinate these effects with the geometry and audio response.

The viewer should be able to see the light traveling through the scene.

Avoid simply animating the entire surface's emission value.

### Localized glimmers

Use subtle, controlled optical accents to reveal curvature and intersections.

Potential features include:

- Fine glimmers on selected folds.
- Brightened regions where waves cross.
- Brief accents along highly curved edges.
- Small emission responses following a passing light pulse.

### Controlled glitch-like effects

Explore subtle, localized effects such as:

- A short-lived phase discontinuity that resolves smoothly.

## 5.5 Make the environment react to music

For example:

## 5.6 Preserve elegance

The scene must remain visually sophisticated.

Do not make every surface move at maximum amplitude.

Do not illuminate every fold.

Do not make every transient trigger a full collection of effects.

Use a hierarchy of dominant flowing structures, supporting ripples, selective illumination, and quieter environmental motion.

The scene should remain compelling during quieter passages, not just during the loudest musical moments.

## 5.7 Resonant Silk acceptance criteria

The scene is not complete until:

- The background is an integrated, animated environment, not a decorative ribbon.
- The environment has visible motion and shares the foreground's visual language.
- The main material has a more convincing translucent or light-responsive appearance.

- Fine glimmers and other accents reinforce the geometry.
- Glitch-like effects, if implemented, are restrained and intentional.
- Audio and MIDI behavior is substantially more expressive.
- The foreground remains visually dominant.
- The scene retains a graceful, flowing identity.
- The live rendering cost is measured and acceptable.

# 7. Performance Investigation — Recover Live Frame Rate Without Blind Quality Reduction

## Problem

All three scenes are struggling to maintain 30 FPS when audio and MIDI are enabled and the output is being sent to the projector.

This needs to be investigated independently of the art pass.

Do not assume that the new effects alone are responsible. The bottleneck may be GPU shading, geometry updates, CPU work, synchronization, rendering resolution, output presentation, or an interaction between several of these.

Do not simply disable audio/MIDI, lower the resolution, reduce the geometry to a crude approximation, or remove the visual effects that make the scenes compelling.

The objective is to identify the actual bottlenecks and improve the complete live path.

## 7.1 Establish a controlled performance matrix

Where the existing application and hardware make it possible, compare the following configurations.

| Test | Scene | Audio | MIDI | Projector/external display | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Each scene | Off | Off | Off or internal only | Establish the rendering baseline |
| B | Each scene | On | Off | Internal only | Measure audio-processing overhead |
| C | Each scene | On | On | Internal only | Measure combined audio/MIDI overhead |
| D | Each scene | On | On | Projector enabled | Measure the actual live configuration |
| E | Each scene | On | On | Projector enabled, representative musical passage | Measure sustained real-world behavior |

Use a reproducible signal or MIDI sequence where possible.

If a configuration cannot be tested, document that limitation rather than inferring its performance.

Also record whether the projector uses a different refresh rate, resolution, scaling mode, or presentation configuration.

## 7.2 Measure frame time, not only displayed FPS

At 30 FPS, the frame budget is approximately 33.3 ms.

At 60 FPS, it is approximately 16.7 ms.

A displayed frame rate alone does not reveal whether the bottleneck is GPU rendering, CPU work, synchronization, or presentation.

Capture the available metrics:

- Average frame time.
- Median frame time.
- High-percentile frame time where available.
- GPU execution time.
- CPU frame/update time.
- Audio processing time.
- Scene update and geometry-generation time.
- Presentation or synchronization waits.
- Dropped frames and frame pacing.
- Memory use and allocation activity where relevant.

Use the engine's existing profiling tools and platform-native diagnostics.

Do not fabricate measurements or treat theoretical estimates as profiling results.

## 7.3 Investigate GPU costs

Inspect the actual shader and rendering costs in each scene.

Potential areas to examine:

- Expensive procedural color evaluation.
- Repeated trigonometric calculations.
- High-frequency procedural noise evaluated per fragment.
- Excessive full-screen post-processing.
- Bloom and other effects operating at full resolution.
- Transparent rendering and overdraw.
- Excessive geometry density.
- Redundant vertex calculations.
- Repeated normal or coordinate calculations.
- Background rendering cost.
- Multiple passes that could be consolidated.
- Unnecessary work on surfaces that contribute little to the final image.

Do not optimize every potential issue indiscriminately.

Use profiling to identify which operations materially contribute to the frame time.

## 7.4 Investigate CPU and synchronization costs

Also inspect:

- CPU-side geometry generation or deformation.
- Repeated allocations.
- Per-frame material or parameter updates.
- Audio-to-render synchronization.
- MIDI event processing.
- Redundant signal evaluation.
- Unnecessary CPU/GPU readbacks.
- Resource uploads.
- Shader or pipeline compilation occurring during performance.
- Presentation synchronization and external-display behavior.

Do not move work between CPU and GPU without understanding the cost on the supported hardware.

Preserve stable timing for audio and MIDI.

Never introduce blocking operations into the audio callback.

## 7.5 Optimize without sacrificing visible quality

Prefer optimizations that reduce redundant work while preserving the rendered result.

Potential approaches, depending on profiling evidence:

- Share calculations between related shader effects.
- Cache stable values and precompute appropriate data.
- Use more efficient procedural evaluation.
- Reduce redundant geometry updates.
- Use lower-resolution intermediate buffers for selected optical effects.
- Use appropriate level-of-detail or quality controls where the engine already supports them.
- Reduce transparent overdraw where it has little visual contribution.
- Avoid recalculating static background information every frame.
- Separate inexpensive persistent animation from costly full-field updates.
- Use existing dynamic-resolution or scalable-quality facilities if available and appropriate.

These are possibilities to investigate, not a checklist of changes to implement regardless of need.

### Preserve the visual result

Do not reduce mesh density, eliminate ripples, remove traveling light, or flatten materials simply because these are easy ways to improve frame rate.

If a new effect is disproportionately expensive, first explore a cheaper implementation that preserves its visible character.

For example, a traveling illumination band may be inexpensive to evaluate as a coherent shader field rather than as a large number of independently updated objects.

A translucent appearance may be approximated using a carefully designed material response rather than expensive multilayer transparency.

A rich color field may be implemented more efficiently by sharing coordinates and intermediate values rather than repeatedly evaluating equivalent functions.

Use the actual renderer to verify that such optimizations preserve the intended appearance.

## 7.6 Treat projector output as part of the real application

Do not treat projector output as an incidental external display.

Inspect whether enabling the projector changes:

- Render-target resolution.
- Swapchain configuration.
- V-sync or presentation behavior.
- Frame pacing.
- Render scheduling.
- Scaling or compositing cost.
- The number of rendered views or passes.

Determine whether the projector configuration is causing the frame rate drop or simply exposing an existing rendering bottleneck.

Do not disable synchronization as a blanket solution. Incorrect synchronization can create tearing, unstable timing, or other problems during a live performance.

## 7.7 Define realistic success criteria

First, establish the intended operating resolution and refresh rate.

Then define success against measured performance at that configuration.

At minimum, the scenes must sustain 30 FPS in the actual live setup, with audio and MIDI enabled, without severe frame-time spikes.

Where the hardware and target configuration allow it, aim higher.

Do not claim that a scene is optimized because it briefly reaches 30 FPS in an idle state.

Report the sustained result under representative live conditions.

If a scene remains GPU-bound after sensible optimization, document the measured bottleneck and the remaining cost. Do not conceal the issue by removing the defining visual features.

# 8. Maintain a Clear Separation Between Art Quality and Performance Work

The art pass and performance investigation should inform each other, but neither should conceal the other's failures.

For each scene:

1. Capture the existing visual result and performance.
2. Implement the targeted artistic improvements.
3. Capture the improved visual result.
4. Profile the updated scene.
5. Optimize demonstrated bottlenecks.
6. Capture the final visual result again.
7. Verify that optimization has not removed or visibly degraded the new features.
8. Measure the complete live configuration again.

Do not optimize against a visually simplified test scene and then assume the final scene will perform the same way.

Likewise, do not accept an expensive implementation without determining whether the same visual result can be achieved more efficiently.

If a visual feature is genuinely too expensive, compare alternatives using actual renders and measurements.

Choose the implementation with the best balance of visual quality, musical responsiveness, and performance.
