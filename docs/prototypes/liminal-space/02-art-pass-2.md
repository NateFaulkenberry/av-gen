# ALL YOU GOT — MUSIC VIDEO

## Detailed Art Pass + Audiovisual Direction Specification

### Purpose

The current version of the **All You Got** music video has begun to establish a mood, but the visual language is not yet coherent enough and, most importantly, the audio reactivity is not actually being expressed strongly enough.

The next pass should **not be treated as a simple polish pass**.

Treat this as a substantial **art-direction and audiovisual-composition pass** whose goal is to establish a much stronger relationship between:

* the music
* the geometry
* lighting
* color
* entity effects
* camera movement
* transitions
* lyrics
* environmental animation
* the emotional progression of the song

The music video should feel **intentionally authored**, like a VFX artist/director made a series of deliberate visual decisions in response to the music.

Do not simply make the world "breathe" in a vague sense and call the result audio reactive.

The viewer should be able to **feel the rhythm visually**.

---

# 1. CORE ART DIRECTION

## Return toward the original POC visual language

Move the art direction back toward the more compelling aspects of the original POC.

The strongest characteristic to recover is the idea of:

> **bright, glowing line-drawn geometry against darker environments**

The world should feel like a primitive, simulated 3D universe.

Think:

* early primitive 3D games
* early experimental 3D animation
* wireframe / line-art environments
* simulated spaces
* geometry constructed from luminous lines
* strange digital environments
* retro-futuristic computer-generated worlds

However, do **not** make this feel like a generic wireframe demo.

There should be an actual visual identity.

The goal is not technological complexity.

The goal is:

> **simple geometry + strong composition + expressive lighting/color + carefully timed animation**

---

# 2. ABSOLUTELY NO LARGE EMPTY SPACES

This is one of the most important corrections from the previous version.

### DO NOT CREATE:

* huge empty rooms
* giant caverns
* enormous empty chambers
* endless hallways
* giant architectural spaces with nothing happening
* disconnected geometry floating around without compositional purpose

The previous version frequently felt like we were simply traveling through:

> long halls → big empty room → long hall → big empty room

Do not repeat this.

## Instead: create a maze of small, intimate spaces.

Think:

* home-sized rooms
* bedrooms
* living rooms
* strange offices
* tiny corridors
* stairwells
* little outdoor spaces
* cramped architectural spaces
* connected rooms
* small courtyards
* strange transitional spaces

The environments should feel **dense with visual information**, even though the geometry itself remains primitive.

---

# 3. FILL THE ROOMS WITH VISUAL INFORMATION

The rooms should not be barren.

Use the line-drawn aesthetic to create recognizable objects and silhouettes:

* windows
* curtains
* wall decorations
* paintings
* shelves
* couches
* chairs
* tables
* plants
* lamps
* strange objects
* architectural details
* doors
* frames
* furniture
* hanging objects
* small environmental props

These do not need to be highly detailed.

In fact, primitive geometry is preferred.

But there should be enough visual information that the viewer can look around and discover things.

The world should feel like:

> **a strange simulated civilization that has been reduced to a primitive visual representation**

rather than:

> **a collection of empty procedural rooms.**

---

# 4. EXPLORE DARKER COLOR PALETTES

Explore darker palettes than the current version.

Dark environments should provide opportunities for:

* luminous line work
* intense color accents
* sudden color explosions
* saturated lighting
* gradients
* emissive objects
* color transitions

Do not default everything to green.

This is **not Glowmere**.

The project can explore:

* deep blues
* purples
* blacks
* dark reds
* dark oranges
* cyan
* magenta
* saturated warm colors
* unusual combinations

Use darkness as a compositional tool.

A dark scene can suddenly become extremely colorful when the music demands it.

---

# 5. OUTDOOR SPACES

The existing GV3 outdoor assets may be reused:

* trees
* rocks
* grass
* terrain
* environmental objects

But they should **not inherit the Glowmere aesthetic**.

Use the assets as raw ingredients for the visual language of this project.

Outdoor scenes should be extremely simple and line-drawn.

Convey:

* horizon
* terrain
* objects
* sun
* sky
* trees
* rocks
* grass
* environmental depth

Think of an early primitive 3D simulation of an outdoor landscape rather than a polished fantasy environment.

Outdoor environments are an opportunity to break up the room/maze structure without abandoning the project's visual identity.

---

# 6. THE WORLD SHOULD FEEL SLIGHTLY BROKEN

Explore entity effects that make the world feel like a **slightly broken simulation**.

Use effects selectively.

Possible directions include:

* data moshing
* geometry distortion
* temporal glitches
* positional corruption
* object stretching
* brief displacement
* flickering
* color corruption
* strange transformations
* frame-like discontinuities
* objects briefly behaving incorrectly
* looping animations
* objects moving in impossible ways

The world should feel alive because **the objects themselves are alive/reactive**, not because we constantly need characters performing actions.

Look at the successful use of timed animations/effects in GV3 and the alien-abduction sequence as inspiration.

The important distinction:

> Do not randomly add glitch effects because they look cool.

Effects should have:

* musical timing
* visual purpose
* compositional purpose
* emotional purpose

# 7. MANNEQUINS / STRANGE FIGURES

The random mannequin-like figure that appeared in the previous version was unexpectedly interesting.

Keep this concept.

Use mannequins **sparingly**.

They should feel strange because they are unexpected.

Potential uses:

* sitting silently in a room
* standing in a corner
* facing away from camera
* appearing briefly in the distance
* occupying a room that otherwise feels empty
* appearing in different rooms as a recurring visual motif

Explore whether a more mannequin-like model can be found.

Avoid something that looks like a generic placeholder human character.

The mannequin should feel like an intentional piece of the world.

---

# 8. LYRICS AS GEOMETRY

Lyrics should eventually become part of the environment.

Do not simply overlay subtitles.

The words should appear **inside the world**.

For example:

> "How little do I know?"

could appear:

* on a wall
* around a corner
* projected onto a surface
* painted onto architecture
* floating as an object
* embedded into geometry
* flashing briefly in a room

The typography should be spatially integrated.

It should be:

* perspective-correct
* angled with walls
* appropriately scaled
* affected by lighting
* integrated into the scene
* sometimes subtle
* sometimes prominent

The words should feel like a VFX artist deliberately placed them into the environment.

Avoid simply putting:

> LET IT GO

as huge flat text across the screen.

The text itself can have personality.

Allow:

* different sizes
* unusual positioning
* slightly quirky layouts
* perspective distortion
* different treatments
* different timing
* interaction with the environment

The text should remain legible, but it does not need to feel sterile.

---

# 9. AUDIO REACTIVITY — NON-NEGOTIABLE

The previous version claimed that the world was "breathing."

This was not sufficiently apparent.

For this pass, **audio reactivity must become visually undeniable**.

Do not simply connect the audio to a generic scale multiplier and consider the task complete.

The agent should deliberately map musical events to visual events.

Explore relationships between:

### Quarter notes

Use quarter notes for things such as:

* geometry expansion
* geometry contraction
* light intensity
* color shifts
* object motion
* environmental animation
* camera breathing
* subtle spatial movement

### Eighth notes

Use eighth notes for:

* secondary pulses
* tension
* repeated text
* smaller object movements
* light flickers
* faster geometric changes

### Claps

Treat major claps as **BIG CLAP events**.

### Instrument changes

Use changes in:

* bass
* synths
* drums
* risers
* vocals
* chorus
* fills

to motivate changes in visual behavior.

The agent should verify the resulting render against the music rather than assuming the signal mappings are perceptually successful.

---

# 10. CAMERA BREATHING

When a quarter-note pulse is requested, do not assume that the only possible response is a pulsing object.

Explore **camera breathing**.

This can be subtle:

* small forward/backward motion
* slight FOV changes
* small positional tweening
* gentle rotation
* subtle camera scale-like movement
* movement through geometry synchronized to the rhythm

The camera should feel like it is participating in the music.

However:

> **Do not turn every quarter note into an obvious camera shake.**

Camera movement should vary by section.

Sometimes the world pulses.

Sometimes objects pulse.

Sometimes light pulses.

Sometimes the camera breathes.

Sometimes multiple elements respond together.

The goal is an authored audiovisual relationship rather than one universal audio-reactive behavior.

---

# 11. BIG CLAPS

Introduce the concept of:

# BIG CLAP

A BIG CLAP is a major visual event.

It should be treated as an opportunity for:

* color explosion
* geometric explosion
* lighting explosion
* entity effect
* scene transition
* environmental transformation
* camera transition
* sudden visual emphasis

A BIG CLAP does not necessarily need to be the same effect every time.

In fact, repetition should be avoided.

Think of each BIG CLAP as:

> **a punctuation mark in the visual composition.**

# 15. IMPORTANT ARTISTIC FREEDOM

The above is the intended direction and timing, but the agent should not interpret it as requiring literal implementation of every example.

The job is to **discover compelling visual solutions**.

For example:

> "growth"

does not necessarily mean "put a tree in the scene."

> "broken simulation"

does not necessarily mean "add glitch effects."

> "celebration"

does not necessarily mean "make everything colorful."

Think in terms of:

### What is the strongest visual metaphor?

### What is the strongest composition?

### What does the music actually suggest?

### What can geometry do that live action could not?

### How can a VFX artist make this musical event visually memorable?

The critic should be used to evaluate these decisions.

---

# 16. DO NOT FALL INTO PROCEDURAL-RANDOMNESS TRAPS

Avoid the tendency to solve scenes by randomly generating:

* rooms
* objects
* effects
* colors
* camera movement
* geometry

Randomness can be useful as an ingredient.

It should not become the director.

Every major shot should have an intentional reason for existing.

The question should be:

> **Why is the camera here?**

> **Why is this object here?**

> **Why is this color here?**

> **Why does this object move now?**

> **Why does this effect happen on this beat?**

> **Why does this scene transition here?**

If the answer is simply:

> "because the procedural system generated it"

then the shot probably needs another pass.

* line-drawn geometry

* luminous edges
* primitive simulated 3D
* restrained environmental detail
* strange objects
* spatial typography
* occasional simulation corruption

* new palettes
* new environments
* new movement
* new visual metaphors
* new entity effects

The viewer should feel that the world is evolving rather than watching unrelated procedural scenes.

---

# 18. SHOT COMPOSITION

For every major scene, prioritize:

1. **Clear focal point**
2. **Interesting silhouette**
3. **Foreground / middle ground / background**
4. **Purposeful camera position**
5. **Interesting geometry**
6. **Intentional lighting**
7. **Meaningful color**
8. **Music-synchronized motion**
9. **Visual relationship to the preceding and following shot**

Do not allow scenes to become visually interesting only because the camera is moving quickly.

A still frame from the scene should already contain a compelling composition.

# 19. REQUIRED ART PASS WORKFLOW

Before making substantial changes:

### Phase 1 — Analyze

Review the existing All You Got project and identify:

* strongest existing scenes
* weakest scenes
* reusable geometry
* reusable environments
* existing entity effects
* existing audio-reactive systems
* existing camera behaviors
* current lyric/text capabilities
* current transition capabilities

### Phase 2 — Critic

Run the creative critic against representative scenes.

Specifically ask it to identify:

* barren environments
* disconnected geometry
* weak composition
* excessive camera movement
* lack of audio reactivity
* weak entity effects
* opportunities for visual storytelling
* opportunities for stronger color
* opportunities for environmental animation

### Phase 3 — Re-Art-Direct

Rework the existing scenes where possible.

Do not automatically throw everything away.

Preserve good discoveries.

Replace weak compositions.

### Phase 4 — Build Missing Visual Systems

If the current project lacks the capabilities necessary to execute this direction, identify those gaps explicitly and implement the smallest reusable systems necessary.

Potential systems include:

* spatial lyric placement
* beat-aware entity effects
* BIG CLAP events
* camera breathing
* color pulse mappings
* geometry pulse mappings
* environmental object animation
* data-mosh/entity corruption
* stronger scene transitions

Do not build one-off hacks for individual shots if a reusable system is appropriate.

### Phase 5 — Render

Render representative sections.

Evaluate them against the music.

### Phase 6 — Critique

Use the critic again.

Specifically ask:

> Does the visual response to the music actually read?

Not:

> Is the scene technically audio reactive?

Those are different questions.

### Phase 7 — Iterate

Continue refining the strongest visual ideas.

Kill weak ideas rather than attempting to polish them indefinitely.

# 20. SUCCESS CRITERIA

The new version should satisfy the following:

### Art direction

* Primitive simulated 3D universe
* Strong line-drawn aesthetic
* Dark environments
* Luminous geometry
* Interesting color
* No Glowmere aesthetic
* No giant empty rooms
* No endless halls
* Dense small environments

### Audiovisual synchronization

* Quarter notes are perceptible
* Eighth-note subdivisions are perceptible where specified
* BIG CLAP events are unmistakable
* Musical transitions create visual transitions
* Camera breathing is used selectively
* Camera movement varies according to musical section
* Audio reactivity is visually obvious

### Environment

* Rooms contain meaningful objects
* Outdoor scenes can be used
* Objects can animate
* World can feel slightly broken
* Mannequins can appear as a recurring strange motif

### Lyrics

* Lyrics are integrated into geometry
* Text is perspective-aware
* Text has visual personality
* Text is not merely screen-space subtitles
* Lyrics appear at the specified musical moments

### Emotional progression

The final video should communicate a recognizable journey:

**winding up → loneliness → tension → growth → reflection → celebration → release → resolution**

# 21. FINAL DIRECTIVE

Do not approach this as:

> "Make the existing video prettier."

Approach it as:

> **"Direct a music video."**

The geometry is the medium.

The music is the timing.

The camera is the viewer's perspective.

The entity effects are the visual performance.

The lyrics are part of the environment.

The colors communicate emotion.

The environments establish mood.

The critic is the visual review process.

Every section should have an intentional visual idea.

Every major musical event should have a meaningful visual response.

And most importantly:

> **If the viewer watches the video with the sound on, they should be able to feel that the visuals are listening to the music.**
